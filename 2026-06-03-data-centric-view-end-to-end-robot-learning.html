This post takes a data-centric view of a modern end-to-end deep learning system that takes many kinds of inputs, e.g. images, text, and measurements, and controls a robot.

The aim is to make concrete what robotics datasets look like, how these datasets are used to train a prediction system, and how the trained model is used for robotics control. I will use concrete examples to show what calculations are performed during training and inference. This avoids heavy notation and abstract language.

I want to make it clear that teaching robots how to do things is centered around data. If we want a robot to learn a behavior, we need datasets that roughly correspond to that behavior. Different datasets covering very different behaviors can work together to train a single end-to-end system. The system can learn internal representations that combine information from the different inputs.

Preliminaries¶

One could skip this part and go directly to the “Examples of Input and Output” section.

I am going to keep things super simple here. I largely follow the notation in WLG+26 but with significant simplifications. I use a single prediction model \(p_{\theta}\) to represent the entire end-to-end system. The prediction model is

$$ p_{\theta}(x_t) = y_t. $$

\(\theta\) represents all the trainable parameters. I use \(x\) and \(y\) as umbrella terms for all possible inputs and outputs. I abuse notation and use \(p_{\theta}\) for both the full model and any individual prediction head. \(x_t\) could contain history prior to time \(t\), so \(x_t\) is really \(x_{\le t}\). The specific output should be clear from context.

The output \(y\) could be anything that we want the robot to predict and control. In most VLA discussions, people focus on actions and how well the robot performs tasks. From a modeling perspective, \(p_{\theta}\) could contain many prediction heads for action chunks, language responses, object detections, collisions, success probability, and so on. Each prediction head needs matching targets and a training objective that updates its parameters.

The input \(x\) could be anything that we could feed into the robot to help with predictions. It could include images, video, point clouds, audio, and language instructions. It could also include the robot’s joint positions, end-effector pose, gripper state, force readings, and contact sensors. For navigation, it might include maps, location, heading, goals, and waypoints. In terms of time horizon, we don’t have to stick to a Markovian view. We can include any history of previous observations and actions. Any information that we think could help the model make better predictions, we could stick into the input. The art and science lie in picking the appropriate set of inputs that fits the resource constraints at inference time and helps optimize \(p_{\theta}\) for its final use.

Notations, diagrams, and any general model architectures could sound rather abstract, making things unnecessaryily complicated. I am going to construct some real numerical examples here to make it easier to understand.

We have options on how to represent some of these outputs. An action could be an absolute hand pose instead of a delta, or it could be low-level motor commands. Navigation could use relative waypoints such as [dx, dy, dtheta]. It could also be a dense occupancy or cost map. The choice of representation depends on data consistency and how the outputs are used by the controller during inference.

Connecting Inputs to Outputs¶

Each input modality first goes through an encoder or projection. Images, robot state, and language all start in different formats. Their encoders turn them into sequences of vectors that the model can process together.

For images, \(\pi_0\) uses the PaliGemma image encoder BBD+24 . After resizing to 224 x 224, one image becomes 256 visual tokens in PaliGemma’s 2,048-wide representation. GR-3 represents the robot state as one state token CCC+25 . Qwen-VLA uses the Qwen3.5 tokenizer from its backbone for language WLG+26 . These published models do not use one shared token width or one literal concatenation scheme. For the simplified example below, assume that modality-specific projections map all tokens to the same hidden width \(D\) before concatenation.

# Camera images
one image:    uint8[180, 320, 3]     -> float[256, D]
three images: 3 x float[256, D]      -> float[768, D]

# Robot state
float[7]                             -> float[1, D]

# Language instruction
"Move the pan to the right."         -> float[L, D]

As a simplified mental model, we say that the encoded inputs are concatenated into one variable-length sequence. The sequence then passes through many transformations inside a deep neural network that combines information across modalities. Different prediction modules use these representations to output different targets.

Each prediction head produces its own loss. We add the losses together, usually with different weights, and run gradient descent through the prediction heads and the shared model backbone. Not every training sample contains the targets needed to calculate every loss. This allows data from specific domains, e.g. action, language, and depth, to contribute to training the same model.

The following subsections make this concrete for action chunks, language responses, success labels, and depth images.

Action Chunks¶
The Flow-Matching Loss¶

Let’s take a look at an example of flow-matching loss on supervised action prediction. Take a batch of observation and action-chunk pairs \(\mathcal{D} = \{(x_t^{(i)}, a_t^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{B}\).

For this concrete calculation, I sample an interpolation step \(\tau \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1)\) and noise \(\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \mathbf{I})\), then form an interpolated action chunk on the straight line between noise and the ground-truth chunk. \(\pi_0\) itself samples \(\tau\) from a shifted beta distribution  BBD+24 .

$$ a_t^{\tau} = (1-\tau)\,\epsilon + \tau\, a_t $$

At \(\tau = 0\) this is pure noise and at \(\tau = 1\) it is the clean action. The network \(p_{\theta}\) predicts the time-dependent velocity that moves \(a_t^{\tau}\) toward the clean action, and we regress it against the ground-truth velocity, \(u_t = a_t - \epsilon\).

$$ \mathscr{L}_{\theta} = \mathbb{E}_{(x_t,a_t) \sim \mathcal{D},\tau,\epsilon} \; \lVert p_{\theta}(x_t, a_t^{\tau}, \tau) - u_t \rVert_2^2 $$

I am abusing notation here: \(p_{\theta}(x_t, a_t^{\tau}, \tau)\) denotes the velocity field prediction used to interpolate actions. This is the function that is often written as \(v_{\theta}\). The target \(a_t - \epsilon\) is the velocity along the straight path from noise to the ground-truth action. During training, the expectation is approximated by averaging over sampled \(\tau\), sampled \(\epsilon\), and the data batch. The magical part is that the conditional flow matching has the same parameter gradient as marginal flow matching LCBH+23 . My previous post explained why that was the case. At the population optimum, integrating the learned vector field transports the noise distribution along the chosen probability path toward the conditional action distribution.

Concrete calculation of the loss¶

The math behind the flow matching objective looks complicated, but the calculation is simple. Here \(a_t\) is the entire action chunk with shape [16, 7]. 16 consecutive action steps, with 7 values to represent the robot state. We sample one \(\tau = 0.25\) and a noise matrix \(\epsilon\) with the same shape. We only show the first row of the calculation below to keep it readable.

a_t = [
  [-0.32752, -0.17010, 0.23136, -0.04102, -0.67765, 0.19326, 0.04846],
  ...
]
epsilon = [
  [0.20000, -0.40000, 0.10000, 0.30000, -0.20000, 0.50000, -0.10000],
  ...
]
a_t^tau     = 0.75 * epsilon + 0.25 * a_t
  first row = [0.06812, -0.34253, 0.13284, 0.21474, -0.31941, 0.42331, -0.06289]

u_t = a_t - epsilon
  first row = [-0.52752, 0.22990, 0.13136, -0.34102, -0.47765, -0.30674, 0.14846]

The network uses \(x_t\), \(a_t^{\tau}\), and \(\tau\) to predict a [16, 7] velocity matrix. The squared L2 loss sums the errors over all 112 values. We do this for every example in the batch and average the per-example losses.

Inference¶

Start from random noise, \(a_t^0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \mathbf{I})\), and integrate the learned velocity field from \(\tau = 0\) to \(\tau = 1\) with forward Euler steps. With 10 integration steps the step size is \(\delta = 0.1\). We evaluate the field at \(\tau = \{0, 0.1, \ldots, 0.9\}\) and produce states through \(\tau = 1\):

$$ a_{t}^{\tau + \delta} = a_t^{\tau} + \delta\, p_{\theta}(x_t, a_t^{\tau}, \tau) $$

For one concrete first step, suppose the first row of the random chunk and the network output are

a_t^0 first row   = [ 0.20000, -0.40000, 0.10000,  0.30000, -0.20000, 0.50000, -0.10000]
p_theta at tau=0  = [-0.52752,  0.22990, 0.13136, -0.34102, -0.47765, -0.30674, 0.14846]

a_t^0.1 = a_t^0 + 0.1 * p_theta
first row = [0.14725, -0.37701, 0.11314, 0.26590, -0.24777, 0.46933, -0.08515]

We do the same calculation for all 16 rows, then repeat it 10 times iteratively. After the 10th step, \(a_t^1\) is the generated action chunk.

Language Responses¶

Language is simpler. Here \(p_{\theta}\) predicts the probability of each possible next token. We train it with cross-entropy loss. For example, a partial response is The man in .... We calculate the loss in the following way.

target tokens                 = [The, man, in]
p_theta(target token)         = [0.70, 0.50, 0.80]

loss = (-log(0.70) - log(0.50) - log(0.80)) / 3
     = (0.357 + 0.693 + 0.223) / 3
     = 0.424

\(p_{\theta}\) outputs the probabilities, while the target tokens come from the dataset. During inference, we sample one token from \(p_{\theta}\), append it to the response, and repeat until we encounter the stop token.

Success Probability¶

In the LeRobot DROID sample, next.reward = 1 only on the final frame of a successful episode KPN+24 . Nonterminal frames and failed terminal frames both contain 0. To train an episode-success head, we could use terminal rows or propagate the terminal outcome across the episode. The head outputs one probability, and we train it with binary cross-entropy.

If we want to predict the quality of an action chunk, we need to define a measurable outcome. For example, success could mean executing the full chunk without a collision, safety violation, or human intervention. During data collection, we save the observation, the proposed action chunk, and what happened after executing it.

success:            true
collision:          false
human_intervention: false
Depth Images¶

Here \(p_{\theta}\) outputs one depth value for each pixel. We train it with a simple mean squared error. Here is the calculation for three example pixels.

target depth  = [1.20, 0.85, 2.10]
p_theta(x_t)  = [1.10, 0.90, 1.90]

loss = ((1.10 - 1.20)^2 + (0.90 - 0.85)^2 + (1.90 - 2.10)^2) / 3
     = (0.0100 + 0.0025 + 0.0400) / 3
     = 0.0175

A full-image MSE does the same calculation over all pixels and then averages across the batch. During inference, one forward pass directly produces the depth image. However, depth prediction can serve as an auxiliary training objective that encourages the shared model to represent scene geometry. We can discard the depth head after training if the robot does not need a depth map during inference. The prediction can still be useful for debugging and visualization.

Data-Centric Learning¶

The idea of auxiliary heads adds flexibility to training \(p_{\theta}\). A prediction module is attached somewhere to a shared backbone. Its loss is added to \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}\), and its gradients update the same backbone used by the other prediction modules. Auxiliary heads can be discarded during inference when their outputs are not needed. This idea appears in multi-task learning, learned navigation, and multimodal representation learning Car97 , MPV+17 , BMAZ22 . GR-3 and Qwen-VLA combine language next-token loss with action flow-matching loss while their action decoders condition on a vision-language backbone CCC+25 , WLG+26 . Additional datasets can contribute when they provide compatible targets for shared representations and the joint training avoids negative transfer. This includes datasets without robot actions, e.g. general vision-language data, human egocentric video, spatial-grounding data, autonomous-driving VQA, and segmentation datasets. Synthetic data can also fit this paradigm when it supplies a target for one of the objectives. Distillation can be another term in the total loss HVD15 . Instead of taking the target from the dataset, a fixed teacher model produces probabilities, logits, or hidden features that we force \(p_{\theta}\) to match.

This is the fun part of modern end-to-end systems. It is centered on learning from data. Data can cover different modalities, perception capabilities, and behaviors we want the robot to learn. Modality-specific encoders, shared representations, and different prediction heads allow one end-to-end system to combine them. What the robot ultimately learns depends on the data, architecture, training process, and prediction objectives.
